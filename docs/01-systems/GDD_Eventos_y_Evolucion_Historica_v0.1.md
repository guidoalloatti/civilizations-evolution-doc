# GDD — EVENTS, OCCURRENCES AND HISTORICAL EVOLUTION

**Version:** 0.1  
**Status:** Consolidated conceptual definition  
**Project:** Historical civilization simulator

---

## 1. General overview

This system will be responsible for generating, connecting, recording and representing the occurrences that build the history of the world.

The goal is for each playthrough to produce its own history, the result of the geographic, economic, social, political, cultural and technological conditions of the civilizations.

The occurrences will not be predetermined by a mandatory historical sequence. They will arise mainly from the conditions of the world, the actions of its inhabitants and the interactions between civilizations.

The following will be combined:
- Possible historical occurrences.
- Dynamic events generated by the simulation.
- Chance happenings and unexpected situations.
- Rare extraordinary events.
- Consequences of the player's decisions.
- Long-term historical processes.

The system must connect apparently independent occurrences and allow them to produce consequences that extend across generations.

**Fundamental principle:**

History will not be a succession of scripted events. It will be the emergent result of everything that happens in the world.

## 2. Generation of occurrences

### 2.1. Generation model

A mixed model will be used:

**Possible historical events**

The system will have plausible occurrences available for different periods and historical contexts.

They will not be mandatory. They may happen if the appropriate conditions arise.

**Dynamic events**

They will be generated from the real conditions of the simulation.

Examples:
- A city grows rapidly due to its commercial activity.
- A family gains political influence over several generations.
- A region specializes in a certain type of production.
- A leader drives a reform.
- A new trade route transforms a region.
- A civilization develops a particular cultural tradition.

**Random events**

There will be a moderate element of chance to introduce uncertainty and unexpected situations.

Chance must not replace historical causes or produce important occurrences without a minimally coherent explanation.

**Extraordinary events**

Exceptional occurrences will be provided for, with configurable frequencies and probabilities.

### 2.2. Causality

Important occurrences must have identifiable causes.

For example:

A drought may cause:
1. Reduced harvests.
2. Food shortages.
3. Rising prices.
4. Migrations.
5. Social problems.
6. Political interventions.
7. Changes in agricultural production.

The system must connect these processes without forcing them all to always occur.

The consequences will depend on each society's capacity to respond.

### 2.3. Scales of occurrences

Events may occur at different scales:

- Individual: people and families.
- Local: neighborhoods and settlements.
- Regional: several cities or territories.
- Civilizational: an entire civilization.
- International: multiple civilizations.

A local occurrence may become a process of international scope if circumstances allow it.

## 4. Balance between positive and negative occurrences

The system must offer a varied historical experience.

Greater relative presence will be given to positive occurrences and development opportunities, without eliminating the problems and conflicts inherent to a historical simulation.

The following will be provided for:

**Positive**
- Prosperity.
- Discoveries.
- Innovations.
- Cultural growth.
- New commercial opportunities.
- Outstanding figures.
- Social improvements.
- Great constructions.
- Diplomatic agreements.
- Periods of stability.

**Negative**
- Crises.
- Epidemics.
- Scarcity.
- Conflicts.
- Disasters.
- Instability.
- Defeats.
- Destruction.
- Political crises.

Extraordinary occurrences may be positive or negative and will have a configurable frequency.

The goal is not to turn the simulation into a succession of misfortunes or into a permanently prosperous civilization, but to produce interesting and plausible stories.

## 5. Chains of occurrences and consequences

### 5.1. Chained events

Events may generate consequences that trigger other occurrences.

The system must recognize causal relationships between different modules.

Example:

**Discovery of a mineral deposit**

1. An expedition identifies a deposit.
2. The discovery is communicated.
3. Economic opportunities appear.
4. An extractive activity develops.
5. Roads and storehouses are built.
6. A new settlement appears.
7. Workers and merchants arrive.
8. The regional economy is transformed.
9. New political and commercial interests arise.

This process may extend over decades or centuries.

### 5.2. Immediate and long-term consequences

Occurrences may produce:

- Immediate consequences.
- Progressive consequences.
- Cumulative effects.
- Indirect consequences.
- Permanent changes.

The player's decisions may have effects that are not evident initially.

### 5.3. Integration between systems

Occurrences must not be implemented as isolated effects.

An important event must communicate its consequences to the relevant systems.

For example, a technological discovery may simultaneously modify:
- Production.
- Economy.
- Construction.
- Transport.
- Society.
- Culture.
- Military.
- Education.

The shared-architecture principle defined in the Technology, Science and Innovation GDD will be reused.

## 6. Historical figures and prominent personalities

### 6.1. Generation of figures

Important figures will emerge through a combination of procedural generation and organic development.

They may appear due to:
- Their individual capabilities.
- Their education.
- Their experience.
- Their relationships.
- Their opportunities.
- Their social context.
- The existing institutions.
- Historical occurrences.
- The needs of the civilization.

Not all important figures will have to appear by a decision of the player.

### 6.2. Categories

The following will be provided for:

- Rulers.
- Generals.
- Scientists.
- Inventors.
- Philosophers.
- Artists.
- Explorers.
- Merchants.
- Religious leaders.
- Reformers.
- Political thinkers.
- Architects.
- Engineers.

Relevant figures may also emerge in fields not initially provided for.

### 6.3. Biographies and personal stories

Important figures will have biographies.

The following will be recorded:
- Birth.
- Family.
- Relationships.
- Education.
- Professions.
- Experiences.
- Achievements.
- Rivalries.
- Relevant decisions.
- Offspring.
- Death.
- Legacy.

Figures may have complex lives and relationships with other individuals.

Their attributes and experiences will integrate with the population, society, politics, religion, technology and culture systems.

### 6.4. Historical legacy

Figures may leave legacies.

Examples:
- A scientist introduces a discovery that continues to be used centuries later.
- A ruler founds a dynasty.
- A philosopher inspires a current of thought.
- An explorer opens a new route.
- An architect develops a style that is replicated for generations.
- A religious leader founds an institution that endures.

Legacy will be important, but its depth may be configured.

A person's influence may diminish gradually, transform or remain for centuries.

## 7. Historical memory of civilizations

### 7.1. Record of occurrences

Each civilization will have an automatically generated historical record.

Two levels will be preserved:

**Full history**

Record of relevant occurrences at the individual, local, regional and civilizational levels, subject to the limits of detail and performance.

**Summarized history**

Record of the most important occurrences, intended for quick consultation and general presentation.

The player will be able to consult both levels according to the complexity options.

### 7.2. Historical encyclopedia

The game will have a summarized historical encyclopedia.

It will include:

- Chronologies.
- Outstanding figures.
- Wars.
- Dynasties.
- Discoveries.
- Historical constructions.
- Cultural changes.
- Religions.
- Treaties.
- Relevant occurrences.
- Territorial evolution.
- Great social transformations.

The encyclopedia will be built automatically from the simulation records.

It will not be necessary for the player to manually write the history of their civilization.

### 7.3. World memory

In addition to individual histories, there will be a general historical memory of the world.

It will make it possible to recognize occurrences shared between civilizations.

For example:
- An international war.
- The spread of a religion.
- The appearance of a technology.
- A great trade route.
- A large-scale migration.
- An alliance between civilizations.

Different civilizations may interpret and remember the same occurrence in different ways.

## 8. Historical traces in the world

Occurrences may leave permanent marks on the environment.

Examples:

- Commemorative monuments.
- Destroyed walls.
- Abandoned cities.
- Ruins.
- Historical battlefields.
- Religious constructions.
- Monuments to figures.
- Architectural changes.
- New neighborhoods.
- Archaeological sites.
- Places of pilgrimage.

Permanence will depend on the nature of the occurrence and on subsequent decisions.

A player may preserve a historical building, restore it, transform it or leave it in ruins, in accordance with the Construction and Architecture system.

History must be something that can be recognized visually in cities and regions.

## 9. Player intervention

### 9.1. Capacity for intervention

The player will be able to intervene directly in occurrences.

They will not be limited to observing what happens.

Intervention may include:
- Giving orders.
- Funding initiatives.
- Modifying policies.
- Supporting movements.
- Making strategic decisions.
- Promoting discoveries.
- Intervening in conflicts.
- Driving reforms.
- Responding to crises.

The capacity for intervention will be conditioned by the type of occurrence and the systems involved.

### 9.2. Important decisions

There will be occurrences that present decisions to the player.

Examples:

- How to respond to a famine.
- Whether to fund an expedition.
- How to act in the face of a rebellion.
- Whether to support a religious reform.
- How to respond to an epidemic.
- What to do in the face of a diplomatic crisis.
- Whether to fund a new scientific institution.
- How to manage an economic crisis.

The frequency of these decisions will be moderate.

The aim is not to constantly interrupt the game with event windows.

Truly important occurrences will be prioritized.

### 9.3. Consequences of decisions

Decisions will have immediate and long-term consequences.

They may modify:
- Economy.
- Population.
- Stability.
- Political relations.
- Culture.
- Religion.
- Technology.
- Territory.
- Diplomacy.
- Historical reputation.

Not all consequences will be known at the moment the decision is made.

The system must prevent decisions from being reduced to clearly right or wrong options.

## 10. Pace of historical evolution

### 10.1. Free evolution

History will evolve in a configurable way.

It may follow plausible historical processes without being obliged to reproduce the real sequence.

Historical periods will be references, not mandatory stages.

A civilization may develop certain institutions before another, remain in certain forms of organization for centuries, or follow particular technological paths.

### 10.2. Pace of occurrences

A mixed model will be used:

- Small, everyday occurrences.
- Gradual processes.
- Occasional important events.
- Rare extraordinary occurrences.

There must not be a fixed number of occurrences per year.

The pace will depend on the conditions of each civilization.

### 10.3. History of other civilizations

The civilizations of the world will have their own historical processes.

However, the level of detail may depend on their relationship with the player.

The following will be prioritized:
- Nearby civilizations.
- Civilizations with diplomatic relations.
- Commercially connected civilizations.
- Rival civilizations.
- Civilizations whose occurrences may affect the player.

This will make it possible to maintain historical autonomy without requiring the same level of detailed simulation for the entire world.

## 11. Presentation of occurrences

### 11.1. Communication channels

Events will be communicated through a combination of:

- Notifications.
- News.
- Historical chronicles.
- Visual representations.
- Indicators within the world.
- Encyclopedia entries.

The presentation will depend on the importance and nature of the occurrence.

### 11.2. News and chronicles

Relevant occurrences may generate narrative texts.

For example:

"After several years of commercial prosperity, the city of Valdoria has become one of the main mercantile centers of the region. The arrival of foreign merchants has driven new constructions and transformed the lives of its inhabitants."

These chronicles will be generated from real simulation data.

They must not invent results or occurrences that have not happened.

### 11.3. Visual representation

Where possible, occurrences will have a manifestation within the world.

Examples:
- Public celebrations.
- Commemorative constructions.
- Arrival of refugees.
- New constructions.
- Processions.
- Demonstrations.
- Military movements.
- Urban changes.
- Religious ceremonies.

The visual representation must be integrated with the animation, figures, buildings and cities systems.

## 12. Navigable historical chronology

The player will be able to consult a summarized chronology.

It will allow them to:
- Review important occurrences.
- Consult earlier periods.
- Identify outstanding figures.
- Consult wars and treaties.
- Review discoveries.
- Observe territorial transformations.
- Consult the history of cities and regions.

The chronology may be filtered by:
- Civilization.
- Region.
- City.
- Figure.
- Event category.
- Historical period.

It will not be necessary to re-simulate the past to consult it: the system will use the accumulated historical records.

## 15. Integration with other systems

This system will be cross-cutting.

It must not become an independent system that duplicates the functions of the other modules.

| System | Integration |
|---|---|
| Geography and environment | Disasters and environmental changes |
| Population and society | Crises, migrations and demographic transformations |
| Economy | Prosperity, crises and opportunities |
| Production | Innovations and productive changes |
| Construction | Monuments, destruction and historical traces |
| Technology | Discoveries and innovations |
| Politics | Reforms, successions and revolutions |
| Religion | Reforms, prophets and spiritual movements |
| Culture | Traditions, movements and transformations |
| Diplomacy | Treaties, encounters and conflicts |
| Exploration | Discoveries and new routes |
| War | Campaigns, victories, defeats and consequences |
| Transport | New connections and logistics transformations |

The architecture must allow occurrences to be generated by the relevant systems and recorded by the common historical system.

## 16. Final vision

After several centuries of simulation, each civilization must have a recognizable history.

There will be founders, outstanding figures, discoveries, constructions, periods of prosperity, crises, reforms, traditions and transformations.

The player will be able to review how their civilization developed and understand why it became what it is.

Two playthroughs with the same starting point must be able to produce completely different histories.

**Design principle:**

*The player will not write a predetermined history. They will build the conditions for a unique history to be able to happen.*
